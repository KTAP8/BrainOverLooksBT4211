# Person 6 speaking script (~2.75–3 minutes, slides 8a–8b, 9–10)

## Slide 8a (~45–50 sec) — Pooled result + FAQ

"Jiayi showed stated and revealed weights diverge—especially for attractiveness. Our business question: if a platform ranked your dates using revealed instead of stated weights, would you say yes more often to whoever is shown first?

We replay each participant's evening: score every date with the six ratings, rank with either their survey weights or our gender-specific revealed weights, and measure yes rate on the top one and top three. Same four hundred fifty-two people as the core model.

Here's the pooled result. Top-one yes rate goes from about eighty-seven to ninety percent—roughly three percentage points overall. Top-three moves about two points. So the headline is modest but positive.

Before we go further, you'll probably have the same question we had: Jiayi's gap on attractiveness is around twenty points—why isn't yes rate jumping twenty points?

Three quick answers. First, is number one always the same person? No—about seventy percent of the time stated and revealed agree on who is first; thirty percent of the time re-ranking swaps who is number one. For that seventy percent, top-one lift is zero by definition.

Second, why are both top-one yes rates still high? We only re-order the same roughly fifteen dates from that night—we never show someone who wasn't in the room. Survey weights still pick someone people usually liked, and many said yes to several dates anyway.

Third, twenty points versus three: Jiayi's twenty is importance on a zero-to-one-hundred scale; our chart is yes or no on the best of the people you already met. Different metrics, and yes has a hard ceiling once both picks are already yes.

So where does it actually matter? Next slide: only the people whose number one changes."

## Slide 8b (~35–40 sec) — Where #1 changes + payoff

"This chart is one hundred thirty-eight participants—about thirty-one percent—where stated number one and revealed number one are different partners.

We're not comparing the same person twice. We're comparing: if the platform surfaced stated's top pick, yes about seventy-eight percent of the time; if it surfaced revealed's top pick, about eighty-eight percent—a ten-point lift. Women in this slice see an even larger bump; men about four points—see the footnote on the chart.

That ten points is where the pooled three points comes from: roughly thirty percent of people times about ten points when ranking actually changes who is first.

This is the customer-value punchline for our project title. People say they want brains and sincerity; choices put more weight on looks. When survey weights and choice weights disagree on who should be first, ranking by revealed preferences changes real yes decisions—not just what people write on the form.

If someone asks what would show an even bigger gap: a full profile catalog instead of fifteen met dates, pickier users, or mutual match instead of yes alone."

## Slide 9 (~1 min)

"So what should marketers do—and how does this translate to a real app?

In the data, the decision is already first contact: my yes versus mutual match. We are not modelling second dates.

Our slides eight a and b are the infinite-feed problem: who is number one in my queue? Use revealed-style ranking when the KPI is whether I say yes to what you show first—especially for users whose stated and revealed number one disagree.

The extension on limited recommendations is a different product move: one curated partner each, optimise mutual yes—not second dates, but both liking each other on the first assigned introduction. There, coordination beats random; bilateral optimisation tends to win over ranking each person alone. Revealed can still beat stated for my top pick while mutual match looks similar across rules in the replay.

For researchers: surveys are hypotheses. For product: don't build copy only from stated weights. For platforms: pick the KPI—engagement on my feed versus mutual matches on scarce slots—then pilot the matching policy that fits. Test yes, match, and retention in production; our numbers are historical replays."

## Slide 10 (~30 sec)

"We're limited to Columbia grad speed dating twenty years ago, observational data, and a historical simulation—not a live test. Halo and robustness checks are with the extensions section.

Bottom line: customers tell you what they value; choices tell you the truth. The gap is large enough to change how we research, design, and rank. Thank you."
